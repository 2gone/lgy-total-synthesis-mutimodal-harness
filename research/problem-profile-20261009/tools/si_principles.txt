Three principles decide whatever the rules above do not name. Each is applied from what the rows themselves say.

What a unit is about decides its label. A PROCEDURE is about the compound it prepares; a CHARACTERIZATION or CRYSTALLOGRAPHY unit is about the compound it measures. When one entry gives two or more labelled compounds, the label is the whole printed string naming them (`20 and epi-20`), and data for each of them that follows is labelled with the one it is headed by. Copy the label from wherever the unit's own rows state it: the title or first line, the labels read from the figure row that opens it (the product, usually the last), or the procedure's own text saying what it gives ("... to afford 18", "Compound 19:" after the workup). When the figure row and the text name different compounds, the product the text says is obtained is the label. Data that follows a procedure with no heading of its own is about that procedure's product. The label is null only when the unit's own rows, and for data the procedure directly before it, name no labelled compound. A name is not a label: where the unit's rows give only the compound's name and no label, the label is null. Nothing outside the unit decides it: not a formula or an identification code, not the order in which compounds appear, not a list given in another section.

Everything said about one entry stays in that entry's unit. A note or remark after the yield, a second scale of the same reaction, the preparation of a reagent used in it, a table of the conditions tried for it, a deposition statement after its data, a footnote that belongs to it: each is part of the unit it is about, and the unit ends where the next entry begins. A page footnote that stands between a procedure and its data does not end the entry: the data after it is still that entry's.

The kind says what a reader would use the unit for. Anything that tells how something was done or tried -- a preparation, a set of conditions screened for a reaction, an attempt that failed, a biological, enzymatic or computational protocol together with the tables of what it gave -- is PROCEDURE. Data that identifies one compound, including a comparison of the natural and the synthetic sample of it, is CHARACTERIZATION with that compound's label. X-ray data, with the caption and the paragraph that describe that structure, is CRYSTALLOGRAPHY. A protocol is PROCEDURE only where the rows say what was done; results given with no account of how they were obtained -- a table or figure of assay values, a drawing that analyses or explains an outcome (a stereochemical model, a mechanism), a caption whose figure is not in the rows -- are OTHER when they stand on their own, and part of the entry's unit when they directly follow the entry they explain. OTHER is also what remains: lists of abbreviations, contents, reproduced spectra, a heading with nothing under it.
