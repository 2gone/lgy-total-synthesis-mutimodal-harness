You are shown one chemistry document after cleaning: every kept block as one row, in reading order, with its handle (block_id), its type, its page, the name of the section it sits under, and its full text. Divide the document into units and answer once, as JSON: a list of units in reading order, each a range of handles (first_block_id to last_block_id, copied exactly from the rows), a kind, and -- where the kind calls for it -- the author's label for the compound the unit is about. The source_phase field of the request says whether this is a main text or a supporting-information document.

What a unit is. A run of consecutive blocks that a reader would want handed to them as one thing. A procedure is one unit from its title through its workup and yield to the last thing the author says about it, even when a page break split it into two blocks -- continues_on_next is only a layout hint that the next row may continue the context. Read the actual text to decide whether the rows belong together; the hint does not establish that they are one sentence or that any source text is missing. The data that follows a procedure (Rf, mp, rotation, IR, NMR, HRMS) is its own unit of kind CHARACTERIZATION, labelled with the same compound, when it begins on a row of its own; when it begins inside the procedure's own row there is nothing to separate and the unit is PROCEDURE. A procedure together with the data that follows it is called an entry below: an entry is one PROCEDURE unit, and a CHARACTERIZATION unit after it when the data begins on a row of its own. Nothing said below about an entry joins those two units into one. A discussion paragraph is NARRATIVE, each paragraph a unit of its own. A heading begins a new unit, except a running header that interrupts a unit and a heading inside a run of OTHER rows, both described below. A figure or a figure caption begins a new unit as well, unless it belongs to the entry it stands in, as the principles at the end say.

The kinds are defined in the kind field of the response schema supplied with this request. Apply those definitions when assigning each unit.

The label. For PROCEDURE, CHARACTERIZATION and CRYSTALLOGRAPHY, copy the label of the compound the unit is about, as the author prints it -- 7, 12b, (±)-3, SI-4 -- from the unit's own rows: its title or first line, the labels read from the figure row that opens it, or the procedure's own text saying what it gives. Copy the label and nothing else: not the name. The label comes from the unit's own rows, with one exception: data whose own rows print no label carries the label of the procedure directly before it. When the author wrote one entry for two or more compounds and printed each of their labels, copy the whole printed string exactly as it stands -- `5a and 5b`, `8a, 8b and 8c` -- and do not choose one of them or drop the rest; the host reads that string as the several labels it names. When the unit is about no compound the author labelled -- a general procedure written for a class of substrates, a table of several structures with no entry of its own -- the label is null. For every other kind the label is null.

What is not shown. Blocks that are empty or that carry only a page number are not in the rows; their handles lie between the ones you see, and a unit whose range spans them is fine. Where the experimental part begins is not asked, because it follows from the units: it is the first GENERAL_METHODS, PROCEDURE, CHARACTERIZATION or CRYSTALLOGRAPHY unit. A main text whose experimental detail is entirely in its supplement -- the usual case for a communication -- has no such unit, and every unit is NARRATIVE, BIBLIOGRAPHY or OTHER.

Rows that interrupt. A unit is a range, and a page can put rows inside it that are no part of it: a running header, or the citations printed at the foot of a page, standing between two rows of one procedure or in the middle of one sentence. Such rows do not end the unit and are not given as units: the unit's range runs across them to where its own text ends. Citations at the foot of a page are a BIBLIOGRAPHY unit only where they stand between two units, not inside one. Interrupting rows never join two units into one: where they stand between a procedure and the data that follows it, the data still begins its own CHARACTERIZATION unit after them.

Copy every handle exactly from the rows; do not compose one. No two units may overlap. The rationale is one sentence saying where the experimental part begins and what marks it, or that there is none.

Six cases the rules above leave open, decided here so that the division does not depend on a judgment call.

A heading is the first row of the unit it heads. It is never a unit of its own while a row follows it under that heading: the heading of the crystallographic section, followed by the first crystal structure, is the first row of that CRYSTALLOGRAPHY unit, and the heading of the reference list is the first row of the BIBLIOGRAPHY unit.

A row that stands directly above a procedure and carries nothing but that compound's label or name -- it is what the page printed beside the reaction drawing -- is the first row of that PROCEDURE, not a unit of its own. The same holds for a figure row, or several in a row, standing directly above a procedure -- the drawings that go with that reaction: the first of them is the first row of that PROCEDURE.

When a procedure's own row already contains the beginning of its data, the rows that carry the rest of that data (a page break cut it) stay in the same PROCEDURE unit. Data is a separate CHARACTERIZATION unit only when it begins on a row of its own.

Discussion is divided by paragraph, not by topic: every discussion paragraph is one NARRATIVE unit of its own. Two rows are joined only when one sentence runs from the first into the second (a page or column break cut it). Do not join paragraphs because they argue the same point.

Rows of kind OTHER that follow one another are one unit: it runs until the next row of another kind, whatever headings or captions stand among them. Do not divide a run of OTHER rows by what they are about, and do not join it to the unit before or after it.

An experimental protocol that prepares no compound the author labels and stands for itself -- an enzyme activity assay, the expression or mutation of a protein, a computational method -- is a PROCEDURE with a null label, one unit per protocol from its title to its last sentence. A protocol that serves one entry instead -- the preparation of a reagent that the entry's procedure then uses -- is part of that entry, as told below for everything said about one entry. GENERAL_METHODS is only the preface about instruments, solvents, purification and how spectra were recorded; OTHER is not used for a protocol that says how something was done.

Three principles decide whatever the rules above do not name. Each is applied from what the rows themselves say.

What a unit is about decides its label. A PROCEDURE is about the compound it prepares; a CHARACTERIZATION or CRYSTALLOGRAPHY unit is about the compound it measures. When one entry gives two or more labelled compounds, the label is the whole printed string naming them, and data for each of them that follows is labelled with the one it is headed by. Copy the label from wherever the unit's own rows state it: the title or first line, the labels read from the figure row that opens it (the product, usually the last), or the procedure's own text saying what it gives (the sentence that says what was obtained, a label printed after the workup). When the title, the figure row and the text name different compounds, the product the text says is obtained is the label. A designation the author builds on a label by a word or a prefix attached to it is that compound's label and is copied whole. Data that follows a procedure with no heading of its own is about that procedure's product. The label is null only when the unit's own rows, and for data the procedure directly before it, name no labelled compound. A name is not a label: where the unit's rows give only the compound's name and no label, the label is null. Nothing else outside the unit decides it: not a formula or an identification code, not the order in which compounds appear, not a list given in another section.

Everything said about one entry stays in that entry. A note or remark after the yield, a second scale of the same reaction, the preparation of a reagent used in it, a table of the conditions tried for it, a deposition statement after its data, a footnote that belongs to it: each is part of whichever unit of that entry it directly follows -- the procedure's, or the data's -- never a unit of its own, and the entry ends where the next entry begins. What is written before the procedure it serves belongs to that procedure in the same way: the preparation of a reagent that the procedure then uses, when the reagent is not isolated and reported as a compound of its own, opens that entry and is part of the procedure's unit that comes after it. A page footnote that stands between a procedure and its data does not end the entry: the data after it is still that entry's.

The kind says what a reader would use the unit for. Anything that tells how something was done or tried -- a preparation, a set of conditions screened for a reaction (a unit of its own unless it directly follows the entry it belongs to, when it is part of that entry), an attempt that failed, a biological, enzymatic or computational protocol together with the tables of what it gave -- is PROCEDURE. Data measured on one compound is CHARACTERIZATION with that compound's label, whatever the method: listed spectral data, a chromatographic purity or enantiomeric excess with its conditions and values, a comparison of the natural and the synthetic sample. A preparation told only by reference to another entry (made in the same way as, by the procedure for) is still how that compound was made: PROCEDURE with its label. X-ray data, with the caption and the paragraph that describe that structure, is CRYSTALLOGRAPHY. A protocol is PROCEDURE only where the rows say what was done or name the entry whose procedure was followed; results given with no account of how they were obtained -- a table or figure of assay values, a drawing that analyses or explains an outcome (a stereochemical model, a mechanism), a caption whose figure is not in the rows -- are OTHER when they stand on their own, and part of the entry's unit when they directly follow the entry they explain. OTHER is also what remains: lists of abbreviations, contents, reproduced spectra, a heading with nothing under it.
